# Marc Andreessen — Core Concepts from the Essays and Talks

A succinct map of the recurring ideas across Marc Andreessen's writing, from the 2007 pmarca blog through the a16z-era essays. Essay titles are noted where a concept comes from a specific piece.

Note on scope: Andreessen never worked at Y Combinator. He co-founded Netscape, Opsware, and Andreessen Horowitz (a16z). His YC connection is limited to guest appearances such as the 2014 Stanford "How to Start a Startup" fundraising lecture and Startup School talks. The ideas below come from his own essays and the a16z thesis.

## What matters in a startup

- **Product/market fit is the only thing that matters.** A startup's life divides into before and after fit. Before it, do whatever it takes to get there, including changing the product, the team, or the market. You can always feel it when you have it, because customers pull the product out of you. (*The Pmarca Guide to Startups: The only thing that matters*)
- **Market beats team beats product.** In a great market, an average team and a mediocre product still win. In a bad market, the best team fails. Pick the market first. (*The only thing that matters*)
- **Startups are a series of near-death experiences.** Expect to be wrong repeatedly and to survive by adapting quickly. Do not start one unless you can tolerate that. (*Why not to do a startup*)
- **The Moby Dick theory of big companies.** Large companies are unpredictable and unknowable from the outside. Never build a startup whose survival depends on a deal with one. (*The Moby Dick theory of big companies*)

## Fundraising and venture capital

- **When VCs say no, it is not a verdict on you.** Venture capital is a portfolio bet on markets and timing. Treat rejection as data about fit with that fund, not about the idea. (*When the VCs say "no"*)
- **Raise money when you can, not when you need it.** The moment you think you do not need capital is the best time to raise. Runway is the main lever against death. (*The truth about venture capitalists*)
- **Venture returns follow a power law.** Roughly fifteen companies a year account for nearly all returns. The job is to be in those fifteen, which justifies a16z's founder-focused, platform-style firm modeled on a talent agency. (*a16z founding thesis*)
- **Founders should run their companies.** Technical founders as long-term CEOs, supported by the firm rather than replaced by a professional manager. (*Why we prefer founding CEOs*)

## Hiring and people

- **Hire for strength, not absence of weakness.** Look for drive, curiosity, and ethics. Do not hire a VP for a function they have never actually run. (*Hiring, managing, promoting, and firing executives*)
- **Beware the professional CEO too early.** Bringing in outside management before product/market fit usually trades vision for process. (*How to hire a professional CEO*)
- **Career planning as option accumulation.** Take risk early in life, stack rare skill combinations, and go where the action is. Opportunity compounds where big change is happening. (*The Pmarca Guide to Career Planning*)
- **Don't keep a schedule.** Work on whatever is most important at the moment. Use structured procrastination and an anti-todo list to track what actually got done. (*The Pmarca Guide to Personal Productivity*)

## How technology reshapes the economy

- **Software is eating the world.** Every industry becomes a software business, and incumbents that fail to become software companies get displaced. This is the a16z investment thesis. (*Why Software Is Eating the World*, 2011)
- **Distribution shifts create new winners.** Each platform shift, from PCs to the web to mobile to crypto and AI, reopens every market and rewards whoever builds for the new distribution channel first. (*a16z essays and talks*)
- **It's time to build.** The West stopped building in physical domains such as housing, energy, manufacturing, and healthcare. Stagnation is a choice and a failure of will. (*It's Time to Build*, 2020)
- **Techno-optimism.** Technology and markets are the engines of human flourishing. Growth, energy abundance, and AI are goods, and the precautionary principle and degrowth are the enemies. (*The Techno-Optimist Manifesto*, 2023)

## The single thread

Find a huge market early, build until the market pulls the product out of you, fund the company aggressively while you can, and keep the founders in charge. The later essays widen the same stance from startups to civilization.
